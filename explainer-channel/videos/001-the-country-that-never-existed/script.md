## Chapter 1 — What did two hundred people find when they landed?  [blocks 1–10]
In eighteen twenty-three, a ship dropped anchor off Honduras and put two hundred settlers ashore in a country that did not exist. They carried printed banknotes they had bought with real British savings. The money was worthless. So was the land. So was the king.

There was no harbour. No capital city. No government building, no theatre, no roads. Only a river mouth, and jungle, and silence. Somewhere in their luggage was a guidebook describing the very streets they were standing on. Every word of it had been invented. [loop planted: what was in the guidebook — closed blk 45]

Within months more than one hundred and fifty of them were dead, and fewer than fifty of them ever saw Britain again. The man who sold them this country was a Scottish soldier named Gregor MacGregor, and he was not finished yet. He had already sold that same imaginary country to the London bond market, twice, for two hundred thousand pounds each time. [loop planted: how a bond market buys a fake country — closed ch4]

What makes this story matter is not that he lied. It is how many clever people needed him to be telling the truth. [loop planted: who needed it — closed blks 52–55] Bankers checked his paperwork. Investors compared his terms. Families sold everything they owned. The system worked exactly as designed. To understand how a country gets invented, you have to start with a war that nobody in London had actually watched. [doorway → ch2]

## Chapter 2 — Who was he before he was a prince?  [blocks 11–20]
Gregor MacGregor was born in Stirlingshire, Scotland, in seventeen eighty-six, and he was not a fantasist. He was a genuine soldier. In eighteen twelve he joined the republican side in the Venezuelan war of independence, and within months he was a general. That same year he married a niece of Simon Bolivar, which placed him inside the family of the revolution itself.

In eighteen sixteen he escaped from Ocumare to Barcelona with a handful of men, slipping past a royalist army that vastly outnumbered him. Bolivar thanked him personally. In eighteen seventeen he was promoted to general of division and given the insignia of the Liberators.

Then, in the same year, he took a Spanish island off the Florida coast with a surprise attack and not a single shot fired. He raised his own flag over Amelia Island and ruled it, briefly, as his own. That is the detail worth holding on to. [loop planted: why that detail — closed blks 19–20] Two years later he seized Puerto Bello, then lost it, and had to run. His talent was never really soldiering.

His talent was arriving somewhere with a flag, a title and total confidence, and watching people arrange themselves around him. So when he sailed home to London in eighteen twenty-one, he was not inventing a new skill. He was scaling one. [doorway → ch3]

## Chapter 3 — How do you build a country out of paper?  [blocks 21–30]
He arrived with a claim. The king of the Mosquito Coast, George Frederic Augustus, had granted him a territory in the Gulf of Honduras. That part was not entirely absurd. Such grants existed. What MacGregor added was everything that made the grant worth money.

He named the territory Poyais, and gave himself a title nobody in London could check: Cazique, the prince of Poyais. A general with a foreign title, a land grant, and a war record was, in eighteen twenty-one London, an extremely believable object. Then he built the country. Not the place — the paperwork. A country, it turns out, is mostly paperwork and confidence.

In eighteen twenty-two he published a guidebook, Sketch of the Mosquito Shore, including the territory of Poyais, printed by a real London printer. It described fertile soil, clean water, gentle weather, and a capital city called Saint Joseph, with a government centre and a theatre.

He commissioned paintings of that capital. He staged public performances of the Poyaisian national anthem. Music for a place that had no people. He opened land offices and sold plots. He printed Poyaisian banknotes and sold those too, for real British pounds. Every one of those props existed to answer one question before anyone asked it. And one group asked it harder than anyone. ## Chapter 4 — Why did the smartest money in the world lend to a fiction?  [blocks 31–40]
The London bond market in the eighteen twenties was doing something it had never done before: lending enormous sums to brand new countries. Between eighteen twenty-two and eighteen twenty-five, Latin American governments raised roughly twenty million pounds from British investors, an astonishing sum for the time.

The Spanish empire was collapsing, new republics needed money, and London had capital and an enormous appetite for interest. Nobody in London had visited most of these places. The whole market ran on documents, reputations, and other people's confidence. [loop closed: which group asked hardest — the bankers, and their only test was paperwork]

Into that market walked a decorated general with a land grant, a guidebook and a national anthem. In October eighteen twenty-two, he borrowed. Two hundred thousand pounds, raised as a bond issue in the name of Poyais. A real debt, for an imaginary country.

In October eighteen twenty-three he did it again. Another two hundred thousand pounds. The market had had a year to check. It did not. A London banking house, Perring and Company, paid the interest on the Poyais bonds, which made the country look like every other borrower. That is the mechanism. Paying interest on time is not evidence a country exists. It is only evidence somebody has money. [loop planted: what happens to a market built this way — closed blk 59]

With that borrowed money he could sell land cheaply enough that ordinary families could afford to sell up and move there. [doorway → ch5]

## Chapter 5 — What happened to the people who believed him?  [blocks 41–50]
Lots as large as five hundred and forty acres, divisible into smaller plots, at prices that no honest colony could ever have offered. Shoemakers, clerks and farmers sold their homes and their tools. Some converted their entire savings into Poyaisian banknotes before boarding. [loop planted: the banknotes — closed blk 49]

In the autumn of eighteen twenty-two, about seventy settlers left England aboard a ship called the Honduras Packet, bound for a capital city. In January eighteen twenty-three, about one hundred and eighty more sailed from Scotland aboard the Kennersley Castle, expecting a settlement already waiting.

They landed at Black River, in Honduras. They looked for the harbour described in the guidebook. There was no harbour. [loop closed: the guidebook] There was no Saint Joseph. No government centre, no theatre, no streets, no colonists waiting. There was a coastline and rain.

Some of them assumed at first that they had simply been put ashore at the wrong place. That hope lasted a few days. [loop planted: what ended it — closed blk 50] They were farmers with no shelter in a tropical rainy season, holding deeds to land that no government anywhere recognised. Disease moved through the camps faster than anyone could build shelter, and the printed money in their luggage bought nothing at all. [loop closed: the banknotes]

By the time a rescue reached them and the survivors were carried away, more than one hundred and fifty of them were dead. [loop closed: the hope] [doorway → ch6]

## Chapter 6 — Why did the victims defend him?  [blocks 51–60]
Fewer than fifty returned to Britain in late eighteen twenty-three, and told the newspapers what they had found at Black River. Here is the part that ought to unsettle you most. When the story broke, some of the survivors publicly defended MacGregor.

They insisted the general had been let down by the men he had put in charge of the emigration party. People who had buried their families in that jungle argued that the man who sold them the jungle was innocent. That is not stupidity. That is the cost of admitting you were fooled, which is often higher than the cost of staying fooled. [loop closed: how many clever people needed him to be telling the truth]

MacGregor moved to France and started again. In eighteen twenty-six a French court tried him and three others for fraud. Only one associate was convicted. MacGregor walked. He went on claiming the title Cazique of Poyais for another fourteen years. He died in Caracas in eighteen forty-five, in the republic he had genuinely helped to free, a general to the end.

Two years after that second bond issue, London's lending boom broke. In the panic of eighteen twenty-five, more than seventy banks failed within weeks. [loop closed: the mechanism] Poyais was never the strangest thing in that market. Only the clearest. Next time: the crash that took those seventy banks with it.
